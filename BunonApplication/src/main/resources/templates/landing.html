Our story

A craft older than the nation

তাঁতশিল্প, the art of the handloom, is one of the oldest living crafts of Bengal.

Ancient times

Fine cotton cloth from the Ganges delta was already traded far beyond Bengal. The river country, its cotton and its skilled hands made the cloth famous.

Mughal era

Dhaka muslin was so fine it was called "woven air" and dressed royal courts. Jamdani, with motifs woven into the cloth thread by thread, flourished.

Colonial decline

From the 18th century, colonial trade policy and cheap machine-made cloth pushed many muslin weavers out of work, and countless looms fell silent.

After independence

Villages around Tangail, Sirajganj, Pabna and Narsingdi kept their looms running. Sarees, lungis and gamchas became everyday cloth for millions.

2013

UNESCO recognised the traditional art of Jamdani weaving as Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Today

Jamdani and Nakshi Kantha are protected as geographical indication (GI) products in Bangladesh, yet the people who make them still struggle for a fair price.

The weaver's struggle

Skilled hands, small earnings

Weaving a single saree can take days or weeks. Too often the profit goes to someone else.

01The mohajan trap

Many weavers take yarn on credit from a middleman and must sell the finished cloth to him at a low, fixed price.

02Rising yarn costs

Yarn and dye prices swing, and small weavers buy in small amounts at higher prices with no bargaining power.

03Copied designs

Power looms and printers copy hand-woven motifs. A weaver has no easy way to prove who created a design first.

04No proof of origin

Buyers cannot tell real handloom from imitation, so honest weavers lose sales to cheap copies.
